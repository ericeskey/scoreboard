**Card 1 — You solved the first problem yourself.** *(We heard)*

Expanded: You did the crunch in two days, found that Power Query "is my friend," and learned your Power BI colleague can automate the rest. We count that problem as closed. This card keeps it on the record.

**Card 2 — The real ask: the scoreboard.** *(We heard)*
Face: *"Where I think I need the real help is around the scoreboard. How do we measure success?"* You asked it twice. Everything past this card is our answer.
Expanded: You asked it twice, unprompted both times. You did not ask for a better dashboard, a cleaner dataset, or a faster refresh. You asked what success means. The essays take that question on.

**Card 3 — The counts don't work.** *(We heard)*
Face: The portfolio's only measure has ever been counts: in, out, approved, active, cancelled. Your own BU leaders reject them: *"I got one project that's this big and another that's ginormous and you're counting them exactly the same."* You agree with them.
Expanded: In three years, no one up to the VPs of R&D has produced anything better. The counts date to a former president of the business, who invented them because there was nothing else at hand. That says nothing about you. Essay I makes the case that the number was never there to find.

**Card 4 — The financial measure isn't available.** *(We heard)*
Face: Revenue attribution for sustaining work is, in your experience, painfully difficult to obtain. When you pushed operations finance to price line-downs and back-orders, they got, in your word, jumpy.
Expanded: We read the jumpiness as honesty rather than stonewalling. The value of prevented trouble is a counterfactual, and finance is right to be nervous about putting dollar signs on events that never happened. The practical consequence: the scoreboard cannot wait on financial data. Essay II starts from exactly this wall. One partial workaround exists; see "The hidden factory" in the case room.

**Card 5 — Two audiences, two definitions of value.** *(We inferred)*
Face: Business units read the portfolio in revenue. Global supply chain reads it in continuity. You watched the same line-down land as an emergency on one side of the house and as routine on the other.
Expanded: The supply-chain playbook for a line-down (cross-train the operators, move them, send them home) is exactly why the event barely registers there, while the BU that sells the product watches revenue and commitments wobble. Our read: this is the deeper reason no proposed measure has ever stuck. Any single number is written in one side's language, and the other side has no reason to trust it. Essay II is about what to build instead.

**Card 6 — Good coping hides the impact.** *(We inferred)*
Face: Asked whether pausing the report-out caused any business impact, every BU leader said no. You also told us how shortages actually get handled: the item comes out of the kit, the customer is told, the order ships.
Expanded: Add the cross-training around line-downs and a pattern shows: your organization copes well enough locally that impacts stop being visible before anyone upstream can count them. The "no impact" answers are honest and incomplete at once. The cost lands somewhere, in goodwill, slack, and accumulated risk, just never on a report. Essay I takes this from observation to warning, with the most expensive precedent on record.

**Card 7 — Nobody asked for it back.** *(We heard)*
Face: Two and a half years of monthly report-outs. Paused at the end of the year. Six months on, no one has asked where it went, and you're left with the honest question: *do we even need it?*
Expanded: Two years ago this portfolio was, in your words, job number one; a corporate VP was alarmed enough about project volume to make it so. Since the split was announced: crickets, again your word. We'd add one thing to the record. A report can go unmissed because the work doesn't matter, or because the report never told leaders anything they couldn't already feel. The essays argue the second, and that the second is fixable.

**Card 8 — Your leaders can't tell you what to build.** *(We heard)*
Face: Your leaders, in your words, "don't know what they don't know in this space." They can't tell you what the scoreboard should show, and everyone agrees the counts aren't it.
Expanded: This changes where the answer has to come from. Ask leaders what they want on a dashboard and you get the counts back, because counts are what people invent when there is nothing else. The workable direction is the reverse: start from the decisions leaders already make (where to put people, what to fund, what to escalate) and build backward to the smallest instrument that serves them. Both essays work in that direction.

## Lock 3 — Bridge

**H2:** The question under the question.

**Body:**
On its face, "how do we measure success?" is a measurement problem, as if the right KPI exists and just hasn't been found. Underneath it is a relevance problem: a better number nobody looks at changes nothing, and the paused report already ran that experiment. So an answer worth your time has to do two jobs. It has to measure something your leaders can't get anywhere else and can't afford to ignore, which points at what's coming rather than what happened. And it has to survive two audiences who will never share a definition of value, which rules out the single number entirely. One essay for each job.

**Essay I intro card:**
Face: *The Report Nobody Missed.* Why work that's invisible when it succeeds can't be made to matter by counting it, why a quiet dashboard deserves suspicion, and what the strongest operators measure instead. You'll recognize the opening; it's yours.
Expanded: One more reason this one belongs to you: when the gloves story came up on our call, you took it over on the spot. *"How else can I show 200 gloves on a proper table?"* The essay ends with those gloves, and with an honest accounting of what they can and can't do.

**Essay II intro card:**
Face: *The Number Nobody Would Accept.* Why the dollar figure you've been asked for doesn't exist and wouldn't settle anything if it did, and how three industries that badly wanted a single number refused it and built something both sides could read instead.
Expanded: This is the practical half: what actually goes on the page, the design rules that keep it honest, and how the standoff between revenue-logic and continuity-logic becomes a decidable question instead of a permanent one.

**Case 1 · The domains — The MDIC Case for Quality pilot**
Setting: Medical devices: FDA, MDIC, and the CMMI Institute.
The move: The pilot first sought one common metric set for all manufacturers. It failed. The template confused people, the burden was heavy, and firms picked metrics to show FDA what they thought it wanted to see. The pivot: four domains (Safety, Effectiveness, Reliability, Availability), with firms submitting the measures they already ran their operations on.
Documented outcome: After the pivot, FDA reported more engagement, more metrics submitted, and submissions more relevant to product quality and organizational performance, alongside faster review timelines and reported gains in trust and data sharing.
What transfers: The nearest direct precedent for your scoreboard: regulator-legible, native to your existing controls, and proof that abandoning the single number raised participation rather than lowering it.
Evidence grade: Industry-consortium primary reports; MedTech-specific; not peer-reviewed.

**Case 2 · The warning — BP Texas City and the Baker Panel**
Setting: Refining, 2005–2007.
The move: None, at first. Leadership watched personal-injury rates improve and read them as safety under control. The Baker Panel found the improving lagging metric had bred false confidence while process risk worsened, and recommended an integrated set of leading and lagging process-safety indicators with executive and board accountability.
Documented outcome: The industry built API RP 754's tiered indicator framework in response; the U.S. Chemical Safety Board later rated progress on the recommendation acceptable, while still noting shortcomings.
What transfers: Your "no backorders this month" is their injury rate: true, and not the risk that matters. Note the shape of the industry's answer, a tiered set of indicators rather than a better single score.
Evidence grade: Independent investigation, primary; the strongest evidence in the file.

**Case 3 · The mechanism — Google's error budget, via the Evernote rollout**
Setting: Internet services and site-reliability engineering.
The move: A service-level objective plus a fixed budget of tolerable failure, agreed in advance. Within budget, product teams ship at speed; budget burned, reliability work takes priority automatically. Nobody argues; the threshold decides.
Documented outcome: The Evernote case reports roughly three months to find targets that fit the service, after which teams used them to steer reliability investment. Google's own material warns that comfort metrics such as raw volume and simple averages soothe rather than inform.
What transfers: The regulated-hardware analog is a bounded exposure budget by product family or business unit, above which discretionary change work pauses. This is the consequence mechanism Essay I lands on.
Evidence grade: Primary practitioner sources.

**Case 4 · The absorbed signal — Honeywell's Safety Observation System**
Setting: Industrial operations.
The move: Replaced a managers-only near-miss channel with a system every employee could report into, in their own language, with corrective actions visible once opened and once closed.
Documented outcome: Its building-solutions business logged more than 82,000 observations in a single year, about eight per employee, while recordable injuries fell from 108 to 54 over three years. Leaders credited the direction of attention, not the volume.
What transfers: Your exclude-and-ship and cross-train-and-move copes are near misses nobody logs. Widen the net to capture the coping itself (expedites, deviations, workarounds, last-time buys) and absorbed strain becomes a leading indicator instead of a silence.
Evidence grade: Practitioner benchmarking (Campbell Institute); named case with reported figures; not peer-reviewed.

**Case 5 · The validation — Cummins' leading-indicator program**
Setting: Heavy manufacturing.
The move: Chose a few candidate leading indicators and tested them against outcomes instead of trusting them. Training hours correlated with incident rate at r = −0.86 across the corporation and its business units, so they set targets, then investigated why it worked. Indicators are reviewed every six months and refreshed annually, on the explicit premise that no indicator stays predictive forever.
Documented outcome: Adoption, target-setting, and a disciplined refresh cycle; the permanent "holy grail" indicator was rejected as a category.
What transfers: The answer to which signals belong on your scoreboard is empirical. Test change-review slippage, NCR recurrence, deviation frequency, and CAPA aging against the outcomes you actually fear, and keep only what predicts.
Evidence grade: Practitioner benchmarking; named case with reported statistics.

**Case 6 · The ownership — USG and Fluor**
Setting: Building materials; global engineering and construction.
The move: USG's plant leaders spent years co-designing their own leading-indicator rating, executed by mixed operational teams visiting peer sites, scored for self-evaluation rather than force-ranking. Fluor rebuilt its HSE audit around leading indicators and, when "management in action" scored weakest, took the finding to executive leadership with prescribed behaviors: weekly site walks, presence at shift-start planning.
Documented outcome: USG reported injury reduction plus broader employee ownership; at Fluor, the weakest audit category became the strongest within six months of the executive intervention.
What transfers: A scoreboard built by analytics alone becomes shelfware. Yours should be co-defined with operations, quality, supply chain, and engineering, every measure with an owner and a response rule.
Evidence grade: Practitioner benchmarking; two named cases; not peer-reviewed.

**Case 7 · The value language — FDA's quality-economics synthesis**
Setting: Pharmaceutical manufacturing, 2024.
The move: FDA assembled the economic case that preventive quality investment pays in operational terms rather than speculative revenue terms.
Documented outcome: One cited site with intermediate quality-management maturity cut defects by more than half, cut waste by three quarters, and redirected a quarter of its staff to other work; a benchmarking study across more than 200 establishments found quality practices correlated with manufacturing and delivery performance.
What transfers: "Capacity freed" is the rare value language both of your audiences can read without translation: prevention that returns people and time to the work the future needs. It is the closest thing in the file to a bridge over the revenue-versus-continuity divide.
Evidence grade: Government synthesis citing secondary examples, plus one peer-reviewed benchmarking study.

**Case 8 · Pricing the invisible — The hidden factory**
Setting: Cost-of-quality and activity-based-costing literature.
The move: Methods for pricing what standard accounting drops (rework, opportunity loss, absorbed effort), including time-driven activity-based costing for estimating what coping work actually costs.
Documented outcome: A consistent finding that traditional accounting misses large categories of quality cost; also consistent reports of managerial resistance when hidden costs surface.
What transfers: This doesn't replace the scoreboard, but it takes the edge off the finance wall from your own experience. When someone insists on dollars, these methods produce a defensible approximation of what the coping costs, without asking finance to invent revenue attribution.
Evidence grade: Peer-reviewed literature; strongest as method, not outcome evidence.
